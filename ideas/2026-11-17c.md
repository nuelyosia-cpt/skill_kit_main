# Ideas: 2026-11-17 (c): great-thinking discoveries

Ten fact-checked stories where a brilliant line of reasoning cracked a problem nobody else could. Each key fact was web-searched on 2026-11-17; sources are at the bottom. Each idea names its ending type: twist, moral or engagement.

## 1. The Man Who Found a Planet With a Pen (Recommended)
- **Hook:** "A planet was discovered by a man who never looked for it through a telescope."
- **Eureka:** Uranus keeps drifting off its predicted path. In 1846, French mathematician Urbain Le Verrier decides something unseen must be pulling on it, and calculates exactly where that something should be.
- **Ending (engagement):** He mails the position to Johann Galle in Berlin. On 23 September 1846, Galle points his telescope there and finds Neptune within about one degree, in under an hour, on the very first night.
- **Why it animates:** Uranus wobbling on its track, a ghost planet tugging it, equations filling a page, a letter flying to Berlin, a telescope swinging to a tiny blue dot.
- **Fact status:** documented (the French Academy presentation on 31 Aug 1846, Galle's observation). John Couch Adams made a similar prediction independently, so add a one-line credit.

## 2. The Table With Holes in It
- **Hook:** "A chemist left empty holes in his table of elements, and dared the world to fill them."
- **Eureka:** In 1869, Dmitri Mendeleev orders the elements by weight and pattern, and leaves gaps where the pattern says an element *should* be. He even predicts the missing one's weight and density.
- **Ending (twist):** In 1875, a French chemist finds gallium and measures its density as 4.7. Mendeleev writes back: "Measure again, it's about 6." The new measurement: 5.9.
- **Why it animates:** element cards dealt out like solitaire, glowing empty slots, a letter "measure again!", a scale tipping to 5.9.
- **Fact status:** documented (Mendeleev's 1871 predictions, Lecoq de Boisbaudran's 1875 discovery and re-measurement).

## 3. The Holes That Weren't There
- **Hook:** "To protect planes, one mathematician said: put the armour where there are no bullet holes."
- **Eureka:** In World War Two, planes return covered in holes, and the plan is to armour those spots. Abraham Wald of the Statistical Research Group points out that you only see the planes that *came back*. The planes hit in the engines never returned.
- **Ending (moral):** It's called survivorship bias: sometimes the most important data is the data you can't see.
- **Why it animates:** a returning plane dotted with red marks, a ghost squadron that never came home, the holes flipping to the "empty" areas. No combat is shown.
- **Fact status:** documented insight (Wald's 1943 report). The popular retelling is simplified: he recommended armour for the vulnerable areas such as engines. Avoid claiming "hundreds saved".

## 4. The Boy Who Weighed an Elephant
- **Hook:** "How do you weigh an elephant without a scale big enough? A child figured it out."
- **Eureka:** Warlord Cao Cao is given an elephant, and nobody can weigh it. His young son Cao Chong puts the elephant on a boat, marks the waterline, then fills the boat with stones to the same mark and weighs the stones one by one.
- **Ending (twist):** It's recorded in the official history of the Three Kingdoms, but historians suspect the story came from an older Buddhist tale from India. Clever enough to travel across countries.
- **Why it animates:** a huge elephant tipping a boat, a chalk waterline, a pile of stones, a beaming child. It reuses the Han China sets from *The Empty Fort*.
- **Fact status:** legend (it's in the *Records of the Three Kingdoms*, but disputed), so the script says "the story goes".

## 5. The Comet That Came Back After He Died
- **Hook:** "Edmond Halley predicted a comet would return, then died 16 years before it did."
- **Eureka:** Halley notices that comets seen in 1531, 1607 and 1682 follow the same path. It's one comet, coming back about every 76 years, and he predicts its return in late 1758.
- **Ending (moral):** On Christmas Day 1758, a farmer and amateur astronomer named Johann Palitzsch spots it right on time. It's been Halley's Comet ever since. Some ideas outlive the people who have them.
- **Why it animates:** the comet's oval orbit drawn in ink, three old sightings on a timeline, a candlelit study, a farmer in the snow pointing at the sky.
- **Fact status:** documented.

## 6. The Map That Stopped a Disease
- **Hook:** "A London doctor stopped an outbreak by taking the handle off a water pump."
- **Eureka:** In 1854, cholera hits Soho. Everyone blames bad air. John Snow marks every case on a street map, and they cluster around one pump on Broad Street.
- **Ending (twist):** The proof was the brewery next door: its workers drank beer instead of pump water, and none of them got sick. The handle came off. Historians say the outbreak was already fading, but his map changed medicine.
- **Why it animates:** black dots multiplying around a pump on a map, a "bad air" cloud dissolving, brewery workers raising mugs, the pump handle being unbolted. No illness shown.
- **Fact status:** documented (Snow's own 1855 report and map).

## 7. The Moon That Ran Late
- **Hook:** "Light's speed was first measured because one of Jupiter's moons kept showing up late."
- **Eureka:** In 1676, Danish astronomer Ole Rømer notices that Jupiter's moon Io appears later when Earth is far from Jupiter. The light just takes longer to reach us. He predicts an eclipse will be about 10 minutes late, and it is.
- **Ending (engagement):** Before him, many thought light arrived instantly. One late moon proved light has a speed.
- **Why it animates:** Earth and Jupiter on their orbits, a light beam stretching like a rubber band, a pocket watch ticking 10 minutes late.
- **Fact status:** documented. The date is given as 9 or 16 November depending on the calendar used. Note: he estimated travel time, and others turned it into a speed.

## 8. The Shadow That Measured a Pyramid
- **Hook:** "A Greek thinker measured the Great Pyramid with nothing but a stick and the sun."
- **Eureka:** Thales of Miletus waits for the moment when his own shadow is exactly as long as he is tall. At that moment, the pyramid's shadow equals the pyramid's height too.
- **Ending (engagement):** No ladder, no rope to the top: just patience and one clever idea, about 2,600 years ago.
- **Why it animates:** desert sun sweeping across the sky, Thales's shadow shrinking to match him, the pyramid's shadow stretching out, a measuring rope.
- **Fact status:** anecdote (preserved by Diogenes Laertius from a lost earlier source; Plutarch tells a different version), so "the story goes".

## 9. The Carpenter Who Beat the Astronomers
- **Hook:** "Ships were sinking because nobody could tell where they were, and a village carpenter fixed it with a clock."
- **Eureka:** At sea you can't measure longitude without the exact time back home. Self-taught clockmaker John Harrison builds a pocket-sized watch, the H4, that keeps time on a rolling ship.
- **Ending (moral):** On an 81-day voyage to Jamaica in 1761–62 it lost only about 5 seconds. The Board of Longitude still demanded more tests, and he needed the King's help to be paid. Being right doesn't mean they'll believe you.
- **Why it animates:** a ship rolling in a storm, a huge brass clock, then a shiny pocket watch, a map with a ship's dot snapping to the right place, stern astronomers in wigs.
- **Fact status:** documented. The payment details vary between sources.

## 10. The Flask That Never Spoiled
- **Hook:** "Louis Pasteur made soup that never went bad, with one bent piece of glass."
- **Eureka:** People thought germs appeared from nothing. Around 1859–62, Pasteur boils broth in a flask with a long S-shaped "swan neck". Air gets in but dust gets trapped in the bend, and the broth stays clear. Snap the neck off, and it spoils within days.
- **Ending (engagement):** Germs come from somewhere, which is why we boil, wash and seal food today.
- **Why it animates:** bubbling broth, the swan neck catching dust specks, a snapped neck, cloudy broth, a modern canning jar.
- **Fact status:** documented. The claim that "his original flasks are still sterile today" is unverified, so don't use it.
