# Ideas: 2026-11-16

Ten fact-checked inventor and eureka stories. Every key fact was web-searched on 2026-11-16 (sources at the bottom).

## 1. The Rubber Man Who Died Broke (Recommended)
- **Hook:** "Charles Goodyear went to debtors' prison chasing a dream made of rubber."
- **Eureka:** In 1839 a lump of rubber mixed with sulfur hits a hot stove. Instead of melting, it chars like leather and stays springy: vulcanization.
- **Twist:** He died in 1860 about $200,000 in debt. The Goodyear tire company was named after him decades later and never had anything to do with him.
- **Why it animates:** sticky rubber goo melting in summer and cracking in winter, the stove hit with a puff of smoke, the springy rubber bouncing, a prison cell door.
- **Fact status:** anecdote. The stove accident comes from Goodyear's own later account. The debt and the prison spells are documented (Britannica, Lemelson-MIT).

## 2. The Chocolate Bar That Invented Your Microwave
- **Hook:** "The microwave oven was invented by a melted chocolate bar."
- **Eureka:** In 1945 Raytheon engineer Percy Spencer stands near a working radar magnetron and finds the candy bar in his pocket melted. Next he tries popcorn, and it pops.
- **Twist:** The first "Radarange" (1947) was about 6 ft tall, weighed about 750 lb and cost about $3,000. Spencer, who never finished grammar school, reportedly got a $2 bonus.
- **Why it animates:** the gooey pocket, popcorn exploding off the bench, a giant fridge-sized oven.
- **Fact status:** anecdote. The patent was filed in 1945. The candy story is the company and family telling; which candy it was and the $2 bonus differ between sources.

## 3. The Mould That Ruined Fleming's Dish
- **Hook:** "The most important medicine of the century grew on a dirty dish someone forgot to wash."
- **Eureka:** In September 1928 Alexander Fleming gets back from holiday and sees mould on a staphylococcus plate, with a clear ring where no bacteria grow.
- **Twist:** Fleming couldn't purify it and mostly moved on. Ten years later Florey, Chain and Heatley in Oxford turned it into a real drug. Three of them shared the 1945 Nobel; Heatley didn't.
- **Why it animates:** a stack of messy dishes, a fuzzy green mould growing in time-lapse, the bacteria shrinking away, an Oxford lab full of bedpans and milk churns brewing the mould.
- **Fact status:** documented (Fleming's 1929 paper). Where the spore came from is unknown.

## 4. The Doctor Too Shy to Listen
- **Hook:** "The stethoscope was invented because a doctor was too embarrassed to put his ear on a patient's chest."
- **Eureka:** In 1816 René Laennec rolls a sheet of paper into a tube, presses it to a patient's chest, and hears the heart louder than ever.
- **Twist:** He named it the "stethoscope" ("chest viewer"), even though you can't see anything with it. The idea supposedly came from children tapping messages through a long piece of wood.
- **Why it animates:** a rolled paper tube, giant cartoon heartbeats ba-dum, kids with a log, a wooden tube on a lathe.
- **Fact status:** documented. The paper tube is in Laennec's own treatise (1819). The children story is an anecdote from later retellings.

## 5. The Bath That Probably Never Happened
- **Hook:** "Archimedes ran naked through the streets shouting 'Eureka', or so a Roman wrote 200 years later."
- **Eureka:** He gets into a full bath, the water spills over, and he realizes he can measure the king's crown by the water it pushes out.
- **Twist:** In 1586 a young Galileo argued the water method couldn't work. The difference is too tiny to see, so Archimedes probably used a balance underwater instead.
- **Why it animates:** the overflowing tub, the naked dash (towel gag), the crown sinking, Galileo's little scale tipping.
- **Fact status:** legend. The only source is Vitruvius (1st century BC); the tale isn't in Archimedes' own works. Galileo's *La Bilancetta* is documented.

## 6. The Apple That Never Hit Newton
- **Hook:** "No apple ever hit Isaac Newton on the head."
- **Eureka:** In 1666, at home while plague closes Cambridge, Newton watches an apple fall and asks why it always falls straight down, never sideways.
- **Twist:** Newton told the story himself to William Stukeley over dinner in 1726, sixty years after the event, when he was 83. The bonk on the head was added by later writers.
- **Why it animates:** the orchard, the apple dropping in slow motion, arrows pointing to Earth's centre, the Moon "falling" too.
- **Fact status:** anecdote. Newton's own telling is recorded in Stukeley's memoir (1752) and by John Conduitt.

## 7. The Snake That Bit Its Own Tail
- **Hook:** "One of chemistry's biggest puzzles was solved by a daydream about a snake."
- **Eureka:** August Kekulé dozes by the fire and sees chains of atoms twist into a snake biting its own tail. Benzene is a ring.
- **Twist:** He told the dream only in 1890, 25 years later at a party in his honour. Historians suspect it might be a joke: four years earlier, a parody journal had drawn the ring as six monkeys holding hands.
- **Why it animates:** glowing atoms dancing in firelight, a snake curling into a hexagon, a ring of monkeys.
- **Fact status:** anecdote, disputed. The source is Kekulé's Benzolfest speech (*Berichte* 23, 1890).

## 8. The Dog Walk That Invented Velcro
- **Hook:** "Velcro was invented by a dog that wouldn't stop collecting burrs."
- **Eureka:** In 1941 Swiss engineer George de Mestral picks burdock burrs off his dog, looks at one under a microscope and sees hundreds of tiny hooks.
- **Twist:** It took him about ten years to make it work. Cotton wore out and nylon worked. And NASA didn't invent it, it just made it famous.
- **Why it animates:** a burr-covered dog, a zoom into a forest of hooks, ripping sound effects, an astronaut's food sticking to a wall.
- **Fact status:** anecdote, consistent across sources (de Mestral's own account, National Inventors Hall of Fame). The patent was filed 1951 and granted 1955.

## 9. The Lamp That Swung Galileo's Pendulum
- **Hook:** "Galileo was supposed to be praying, but he was timing a lamp with his own pulse."
- **Eureka:** Around 1583, in Pisa Cathedral, a lamp swings. Big swing or small swing, it takes the same time.
- **Twist:** The famous lamp that tourists see today was hung there after Galileo's time. The story first appears in a biography by his student Viviani, written decades later.
- **Why it animates:** a huge cathedral with a swinging lamp, a pulse beating in sync, a ghostly pendulum clock appearing.
- **Fact status:** legend. Viviani's account (1654). The lamp problem was raised by the historian Favaro (Museo Galileo).

## 10. The Man Who Measured Earth With a Shadow
- **Hook:** "2,200 years ago, a librarian measured the whole Earth using a stick and a well."
- **Eureka:** At noon on midsummer the sun shines straight down a well in Syene, but a stick in Alexandria casts a shadow of about 7°, roughly 1/50 of a circle.
- **Twist:** 50 × 5,000 stadia ≈ 250,000 stadia, depending on the stadion, within a few percent of the real figure, centuries before anyone sailed around the world.
- **Why it animates:** a sunbeam diving into a well, two sticks and two shadows, the globe sliced like a pizza into 50 pieces, a camel pacing out the distance.
- **Fact status:** documented-ish. Eratosthenes' own book is lost; the method survives in Cleomedes' later summary, which simplifies it.
