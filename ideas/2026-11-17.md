# Ideas: 2026-11-17

Ten fact-checked stories. Each key fact was web-searched on 2026-11-17; sources are at the bottom. Each idea names its ending type: twist, moral or engagement (SKILL.md stage 2).

## 1. The Dragon That Felt an Earthquake First (Recommended)
- **Hook:** "Nearly 1,900 years ago, a bronze dragon spat out a ball, and nobody in the room felt a thing."
- **Eureka:** In 132 AD, Zhang Heng presents a bronze jar to the Han court at Luoyang. Eight dragons each hold a ball over a bronze toad, and a pendulum inside tips the dragon that faces a distant quake.
- **Ending (engagement):** Courtiers laughed when the western dragon dropped its ball. Days later a rider galloped in: a big earthquake had hit Longxi, hundreds of miles west. Nobody has ever managed to rebuild the inside exactly.
- **Why it animates:** eight dragons and eight open-mouthed toads; a ball dropping, *clang*; the mocking courtiers; a messenger galloping across a map. It also reuses the Han China sets and costumes from *The Empty Fort*.
- **Fact status:** documented, in the *Book of Later Han* (compiled in the 5th century). The Longxi date varies between sources (often given as 138 AD), so the script says "a few years later".

## 2. The Glue That Was Too Weak
- **Hook:** "The Post-it Note was made from a glue that failed."
- **Eureka:** In 1968, 3M chemist Spencer Silver tries to make a super-strong glue and gets one that barely sticks. Years later, Art Fry's paper bookmarks keep falling out of his choir hymnal, and he remembers Silver's "useless" glue.
- **Ending (moral):** Silver spent years presenting a glue nobody wanted, and it took a choir singer to find its use. A "failure" is sometimes just an idea waiting for its problem.
- **Why it animates:** a glue test that peels off weakly; bookmarks fluttering out of a hymnal mid-song; yellow notes multiplying across a whole office.
- **Fact status:** documented (3M's company history and the inventors' own accounts). Sources give 1973 or 1974 for Fry's idea.

## 3. The Empty Tank That Was Too Heavy
- **Hook:** "Your non-stick pan exists because a gas tank wouldn't empty."
- **Eureka:** On 6 April 1938, at DuPont, Roy Plunkett opens a cylinder of refrigerant gas and nothing comes out, yet it still weighs as much as a full one. He saws it open and finds a slippery white powder: PTFE, later called Teflon.
- **Ending (engagement):** He was trying to make a better fridge gas, and he made one of the slipperiest materials ever found.
- **Why it animates:** a hissing valve that goes silent, the tank on a scale, a hacksaw, white powder pouring out, an egg sliding across a pan.
- **Fact status:** documented (Science History Institute, Lemelson-MIT).

## 4. The Hand That Saw Her Own Skeleton
- **Hook:** "The first X-ray of a human was of a wife's hand, and supposedly she didn't like it."
- **Eureka:** In November 1895 at Würzburg, Wilhelm Röntgen covers a glowing tube with black card, yet a screen across the room still glows. Invisible rays are passing through solid things.
- **Ending (twist):** He X-rays his wife Anna Bertha's hand, wedding ring and all. The story goes that she said, "I have seen my death."
- **Why it animates:** a dark lab, a ghostly green glow on the screen, bones appearing inside a hand, the ring floating on bare bone.
- **Fact status:** documented discovery. Her quote is an anecdote; the date and which hand vary between sources.

## 5. The Milkmaid Who Beat Smallpox
- **Hook:** "The word 'vaccine' comes from the Latin word for cow."
- **Eureka:** Milkmaids who caught mild cowpox never seemed to get smallpox. On 14 May 1796, Edward Jenner takes cowpox from milkmaid Sarah Nelmes and gives it to 8-year-old James Phipps. Later, smallpox can't touch him.
- **Ending (engagement):** Vacca means "cow". A cow named Blossom is behind the word in billions of vaccinations.
- **Why it animates:** a milkmaid and a cow, a country doctor's notebook, the boy with a thumbs up, the word "VACCA" morphing into "VACCINE".
- **Fact status:** documented (Jenner's own 1798 report). Note: others such as the farmer Benjamin Jesty tried cowpox first; Jenner was the first to test it scientifically.

## 6. The Doctor Who Said "Wash Your Hands"
- **Hook:** "A doctor once saved hundreds of mothers, and other doctors were offended."
- **Eureka:** In 1847, at Vienna General Hospital, the doctors' maternity ward has about three times the deaths of the midwives' ward. Ignaz Semmelweis realises doctors carry something on their hands, and he orders washing in chlorinated lime.
- **Ending (moral):** Deaths in his ward dropped from about 18 % to about 2 %. Many colleagues refused to believe it. He was right 20 years before germ theory caught up, and being right isn't always enough to be believed.
- **Why it animates:** two wards side by side, a bowl of chlorine water, a chart line diving, offended doctors with crossed arms.
- **Fact status:** documented (clinic records). Keep it light: no illness shown, and only "he never saw it accepted" for his death.

## 7. The Alchemist Who Boiled 5,000 Litres of Pee
- **Hook:** "One element was discovered by boiling buckets of urine."
- **Eureka:** In Hamburg in 1669, Hennig Brand hunts for the philosopher's stone, which was supposed to turn things into gold. He boils down huge amounts of urine and heats the sludge for hours, and something glows in the dark: phosphorus, "cold fire".
- **Ending (twist):** He never made gold. But the light-bearer he found is now in matches, fertiliser and your DNA.
- **Why it animates:** bubbling cauldrons, wrinkled noses, the glowing green blob in a dark lab, a match flaring at the end.
- **Fact status:** documented (the discovery date and place). The amount of urine is a later estimate, so say "about".

## 8. The Woman Who Invented the Windshield Wiper
- **Hook:** "Every car has her invention, and she never made a cent from it."
- **Eureka:** On a snowy New York streetcar, Mary Anderson watches the driver open his window to clear the glass. Back home she designs a hand lever that swings a rubber blade across the outside: patented 10 November 1903.
- **Ending (moral):** Companies said it had "no commercial value". Her patent expired in 1920, just as wipers became standard. Ahead of your time still counts.
- **Why it animates:** snow piling on the glass, a shivering driver leaning out, the blade swish-swish, a highway of modern cars with wipers.
- **Fact status:** documented (US patent 743,801). The year of the streetcar ride is 1902 or 1903, depending on the source.

## 9. The Printer 400 Years Before Gutenberg
- **Hook:** "Movable type was invented in China, out of baked clay, four centuries before Gutenberg."
- **Eureka:** Around 1040, a commoner named Bi Sheng carves single characters into clay, bakes them hard and arranges them on an iron plate, so any page can be built from the same reusable pieces.
- **Ending (engagement):** We know about him only because one scholar, Shen Kuo, wrote it down. One paragraph saved a whole invention.
- **Why it animates:** tiny clay blocks baked in a kiln, characters clicking into a frame, pages flying off, Shen Kuo's brush writing it down.
- **Fact status:** documented (Shen Kuo's *Dream Pool Essays*, 1088).

## 10. The Kite That Maybe Never Flew
- **Hook:** "Benjamin Franklin's famous kite in a storm might never have flown."
- **Eureka:** In 1752, the story goes, Franklin flies a silk kite into a storm cloud with a key on the string, and sparks jump to his knuckle: lightning is electricity.
- **Ending (twist):** Franklin never wrote that he did it himself. The famous version comes from Joseph Priestley, 15 years later, and the paintings show a lightning strike that would have killed him.
- **Why it animates:** a dark storm, the kite swooping, string fibres standing up, a tiny spark at the key, a painting being "corrected".
- **Fact status:** anecdote, disputed (Priestley 1767; historians like Tom Tucker doubt it).
