The Mona Lisa wasn't the most famous painting in the world, until somebody stole it.
Paris, August 1911. On a Monday, when the Louvre is closed, a man in a white worker's smock walks calmly out of the museum. Under the smock is Leonardo da Vinci's portrait.
His name is Vincenzo Peruggia. He had worked at the Louvre as a handyman, so the night before, he simply hid in a storage cupboard and waited.
His plan was almost too simple. He dressed exactly like the staff, so nobody looked twice.
When the guards noticed the empty wall, they assumed she was being photographed. It took more than a day before anyone raised the alarm.
Then the world went crazy. Her face was printed in newspapers everywhere. The museum closed for a week. When it reopened, crowds queued for hours, just to stare at four empty hooks.
The police questioned everyone. A famous poet was arrested. Even a young painter named Pablo Picasso was brought in for questioning.
Meanwhile, the real thief kept her hidden in a trunk in his small Paris room, for two years.
He was finally caught in Florence, trying to sell her.
But by then, everybody knew her face.
The thief didn't steal the Mona Lisa's fame. He created it.
