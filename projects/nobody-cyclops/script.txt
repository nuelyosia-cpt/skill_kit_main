The smartest trick in Greek myth was a fake name.
In Homer's ancient poem, Odysseus is sailing home from the Trojan War. He and twelve men enter a cave full of cheese and sheep.
Then the owner comes home. Polyphemus, a one-eyed giant as tall as a house. He seals the door with a boulder twenty-two wagons couldn't move. And the sailors are dinner.
They can't fight him. Even if they won, nobody else could move that rock.
So Odysseus makes a plan. He sharpens a stake and hides it. He pours the giant strong wine. When the giant asks his name, Odysseus is ready. "My name is Nobody."
That night, they strike the giant's one eye with the burning stake. He screams for help. The other Cyclopes shout, "Who's hurting you?"
"Nobody is hurting me!"
So everyone goes back to bed. That's why he lied: a cry for help that says nothing.
At dawn, the blind giant rolls the stone aside and feels each sheep's back. But the men are clinging underneath their bellies.
It works. But safe on his ship, Odysseus can't resist. He shouts his real name.
The giant's father is Poseidon, god of the sea. His curse keeps Odysseus from home for ten years.
One proud sentence ruined a perfect plan.
